Movement One
3:1–26

The Healing & the Sermon

What Peter actually had to give

Peter and John are on their way into the Temple for the hour of prayer when a man who has never walked in his life asks them for money. Peter's answer is the whole scene in miniature: "I have no silver and gold, but I give you what I have." He doesn't have money. He has something worth infinitely more, and he doesn't hesitate to give it.

Acts 3:6

"I have no silver and gold, but I give you what I have; in the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth, walk."

In the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth, the man's feet and ankles are made strong, and he goes into the Temple with them, walking and leaping and praising God. The crowd that gathers around them does so in Solomon's Portico, and the location isn't incidental. Peter and John are continuing their ministry in the very place Jesus once stood and made his own claims (John 10:22–23), a place where all with ears could hear, not just an elect few.

Peter's first move in the sermon that follows is to deflect. Why are you staring at us, he asks, as if our own power made this man walk? Everything that happened came from the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, who glorified his servant Jesus, the very one the crowd handed over and denied, even after Pilate had decided to let him go. They asked for a murderer instead, and killed, in Peter's words, "the Author of life." God raised him from the dead anyway.

Word Study ἀρχηγός archēgos ar-khay-GOS G747
author, originator leader, prince pioneer

"Author of life" (3:15) translates archēgos, a word that means far more than "writer." It's the leader who goes first, the source a whole line traces back to. Peter uses the same word for Jesus in Acts 5:31 ("Prince and Savior"), and Hebrews later calls him the archēgos of our salvation and of our faith (Hebrews 2:10, 12:2). The irony Peter is pointing at directly: they killed the source of life itself, and it didn't take.

Peter saying that they acted in ignorance is a question most readers will have. How could these people, who had just given Jesus up weeks before, be expected to turn back to him? Didn't they already see everything they needed to see, and still reject him? The answer is that failure doesn't cut us off from Christ. Peter himself denied him three times. It's repenting and returning, admitting you were wrong, that defines who you will be going forward.

Acts 3:17

"And now, brethren, I know that you acted in ignorance, as did also your rulers."

It's fitting that Peter is the one giving this sermon, because he was the apostle who said by no means would he ever deny Jesus, and then did, three times. Peter knows how it feels to be so ashamed you think you aren't worth saving anymore, which means he also knows exactly how powerful it was for Jesus to forgive him anyway. He offers the same forgiveness to the crowd, knowing that someone who has fallen and been helped back up can sometimes end up stronger because of it.

Acts 3:25–26

"You are the sons of the prophets and of the covenant which God gave to your fathers... God, having raised up his servant, sent him to you first, to bless you in turning every one of you from your wickedness."

Commentary

Solomon's Portico was a covered colonnade along the eastern edge of the Temple Mount, in the outer Court of the Gentiles, the most public, most accessible part of the whole complex. Ancient tradition (Josephus, Antiquities 20.221) held it was the one part of the Temple structure old enough to date back to Solomon's original building. It's also where Jesus himself taught and was directly challenged about his identity: "How long will you keep us in suspense? If you are the Christ, tell us plainly" (John 10:24). Peter isn't picking a random location. He's standing exactly where that question was left open, and answering it.

This also isn't the last time it shows up. Acts 5:12 shows the apostles continuing to gather there regularly afterward. This scene is effectively the opening of that becoming the early Church's standing public meeting place, while they're still fully participating in Temple life, not breaking from it.

Reflection — Movement One

Responses save to your Study Journal

Peter had no silver or gold to give, only something worth more. What's something you have to give someone that isn't money, time, or a skill, but has still cost you something to give freely?

Peter's own three denials are what let him preach forgiveness credibly. Is there a failure in your own life that, once you stopped hiding from it, became something you could actually use to help someone else?

Movement Two
4:1–22

Before the Council

No other name under heaven

The sermon doesn't get to finish peacefully. Priests, the captain of the Temple, and the Sadducees arrest Peter and John before the day is out, unhappy that they're preaching the resurrection through Jesus. It doesn't slow anything down. Many who heard the message believe anyway, and the number of men alone grows to about five thousand.

The next day, the rulers, elders, and scribes, including the high priest Annas and Caiaphas, demand to know by what power they did this. The apostles aren't here to spread their own names far and wide, or to claim that the kingdom is now theirs. They plainly explain that everything they are doing comes from Jesus Christ himself. The healing and the miracles aren't a power passed down to them as the next phase of Christ's plan. They are borrowed power from the Author of Life.

Acts 4:12

"And there is salvation in no one else, for there is no other name under heaven given among men by which we must be saved."

Word Study παρρησία parrhēsia par-hray-SEE-ah G3954
boldness confidence freedom of speech

Parrhēsia comes from pan (all) and rhēsis (speech): saying everything, holding nothing back. It's the word the council notices in Peter and John (4:13), the word the whole community prays for afterward (4:29), and the word Luke uses to describe what they get (4:31). It shows up again later in Hebrews 4:16, describing how believers can approach God's throne itself: with boldness. Same word, same posture.

We often think it's the most well-read, highest-IQ individual who has all the right answers, but it's important to understand that faith doesn't ask you to be a genius. God doesn't require that you understand how DNA works, or that you can crack the code of how the resurrection took place, in order to be saved. It's the people humble enough to say "I don't know how this works, but I believe God created it, and me" who are truly blessed. Peter wasn't coming to the crowd with a thesis statement on how Christ rose from the dead, or an explanation of how miracles were performed. He simply asked people to be humble enough to admit their mistakes, repent, and come back to Christ.

Acts 4:13

"Now when they saw the boldness of Peter and John, and perceived that they were uneducated, common men, they wondered; and they recognized that they had been with Jesus."

The council isn't denying the evidence that a miracle has just taken place. They don't dispute that this man now jumping for joy in the Temple was a cripple the day before. The same man had been coming to beg at the Temple every day, asking for alms. Surely the Temple leaders had seen him, and maybe flipped a coin or two in his direction. They didn't say to Peter, "so tell us what the trick was. Is he a stunt double? Or was he just faking his paralysis this whole time?" They know what just happened was nothing other than a miracle, but to admit that means accepting that Peter and John have some kind of power, and they want to know where it comes from.

When Peter and John tell the Temple leaders that this miracle came from Jesus Christ of Nazareth, they don't deny it or call them liars either. They simply tell Peter and John to speak of it to no one and to stop spreading Jesus's name. They ask them to hide the truth because it would be embarrassing for their people to know that the man they handed over to be killed weeks before was exactly who he said he was, and they had failed to kill him.

So Peter and John give their response, short and to the point: you're asking us to choose between obeying you and obeying God. You decide whether that's the right choice, but we know where this miracle came from, and we cannot stop speaking about what we've seen and heard.

Acts 4:19–20

"Whether it is right in the sight of God to listen to you rather than to God, you must judge; for we cannot but speak of what we have seen and heard."

Reflection — Movement Two

The council's real obstacle wasn't the evidence, it was what speaking the truth would cost them. Where in your own life do you already know what's true, but the price of saying so out loud is what's actually holding you back?

Peter and John were "uneducated, common men," and it didn't disqualify them. What credential do you think you're missing before you'd feel ready to talk about your own faith?

Movement Three
4:23–37

The Prayer & the Community

Boldness, not safety

Released, Peter and John go back to their own and report everything. The whole community prays together, naming Herod, Pontius Pilate, the Gentiles, and the people of Israel as the ones gathered against Jesus, applying the words of Psalm 2 to what just happened. Similar to Jesus allowing Judas to follow him, knowing he'd betray him, God doesn't need evil to accomplish his plan. He permits human beings the freedom to act, even to do wrong, and in his providence he's able to weave even those choices into something redemptive. Judas's betrayal wasn't a good act, and neither was Herod's or Pilate's part in the crucifixion, but God's power is big enough to bring life out of exactly what evil intended for harm.

Acts 4:27–28

"Truly in this city there were gathered together against thy holy servant Jesus... both Herod and Pontius Pilate, with the Gentiles and the peoples of Israel, to do whatever thy hand and thy plan had predestined to take place."

Jesus warned the disciples that they would be hated because of him. They knew exactly what they were getting into, and that there wouldn't be an easy path to spreading the Gospel. So when they pray after being threatened, they don't pray for the threats to go away. They pray for boldness.

Acts 4:29, 31

"And now, Lord, look upon their threats, and grant to thy servants to speak thy word with all boldness... And when they had prayed, the place in which they were gathered together was shaken; and they were all filled with the Holy Spirit and spoke the word of God with boldness."

Word Study ὁμοθυμαδόν homothymadon ho-mo-thoo-mah-DON G3661
with one accord of one mind together, as one

"They lifted their voices together" (4:24) translates homothymadon, from homos (same) and thymos (passion, mind). It's Luke's favorite word for how this community moves: not a crowd of individuals who happen to agree, but one body praying a single prayer. He uses it again for the upper room in 1:14, the daily Temple gatherings in 2:46, and the community in 5:12. It's doing a lot of quiet work every time it shows up.

This was a group of people not after wealth or notoriety. They shared so that no one in their order could be called greater than another.

Acts 4:32, 34–35

"Now the company of those who believed were of one heart and soul, and no one said that any of the things which he possessed was his own, but they had everything in common... distribution was made to each as any had need."

One more name worth remembering here: Barnabas, introduced for the first time as an example of this generosity, selling a field and laying the money at the apostles' feet. Keep an eye on him. His story isn't finished.

Reflection — Movement Three

The early Church prayed for boldness, not for their problems to disappear. What would it look like to pray that way about something you're currently facing?

No one in the community claimed to be greater because of what they gave. Where in your own life is generosity tangled up with wanting credit for it?
