Romans 6–7

The Fight Inside

3 Movements Greek Word Studies Church Fathers CCC References Reflection Prompts
Book Context — Romans ▼

Author

Paul of Tarsus, dictated to Tertius

Written

c. AD 57, from Corinth

Audience

Jewish and Gentile Christians in Rome, meeting in house churches

Political Context

Emperor Claudius had expelled Jews from Rome (c. AD 49); many had returned under Nero. The community was fractured along ethnic and theological lines.

Chapters 6–7 in the Letter

After establishing that grace is the answer (chs. 1–5), Paul addresses what a grace-covered life actually looks like from the inside — harder and messier than expected.

Translation

Revised Standard Version, Catholic Edition (RSV-CE)

Romans 6–7 is the bridge most readers rush across to get to chapter 8. Paul slows down here on purpose. He has to answer two questions before the Spirit chapter makes any sense: Does grace permit sin? And is the law the problem? The answers are no and no — but getting there takes two full chapters.

Movement One
Romans 6:1–14

Dead to Sin, Alive to God

Baptism was a burial, not a ceremony

"How can we who died to sin still live in it?" Romans 6:2

Paul tells us up to this point that God's grace saves us from our sins. So, we have a hall pass to sin because we're already saved by Christ's sacrifice, right? Unfortunately, no.

Romans 6 starts by telling us that we have died to sin and that through baptism we are resurrected with Christ, and sin no longer has dominion over us. Dominion is an important word here. The Greek word for it is kyrieuokoo-ree-EW-oh, which comes from the root word kyrios, meaning lord. Sin no longer having dominion over us doesn't mean it just goes away and temptation no longer exists. It would be great if that were true, but unfortunately it's not that simple. Paul uses kyrieuo to say that sin had been our lord since Adam and Eve were kicked out of Eden. Not just a temptation in the back of our mind, but literally the lord we gave worship to.

This can be confusing because obviously humans weren't literally worshiping sin in the centuries before Christ came. Romans 6:15–23 goes on to explain what was truly happening. Humans were not actively intending to sin — in fact some believed they were doing exactly what God was calling them to do. But in trying to live a life that served God, many became slaves to sin. The law that was meant to teach people how to avoid sinfulness instead became their form of worship. "I follow the law, therefore I am a good person." By being prideful that we follow the law to the tee, we're actually coveting the law itself over actual relationship with God. That is the paradox of the law.

That does not mean that the law was bad or that God made an error by giving humans the law of Moses. The problem was that sin still lorded over people in those days, and humans in our broken nature turned what was meant for good into something that punished those who disobeyed. Instead of showing people how to be righteous, it became a tool for separating the "good" people from the "bad" — which is the opposite of what God wants and the opposite of what Jesus did on earth.

The Pharisees were a perfect example of this, and one of my favorite examples of it playing out in real time comes from John 8:1–11. A woman caught in adultery — breaking one of the laws of Moses — is called to be stoned by the Pharisees. She has broken the law, and the law demands she die for it. What Jesus does next quietly explains everything Paul is arguing up to this point. The Pharisees think they have him trapped. He can either agree the law demands her death, or deny what the law says and give her a pass. No middle ground, or so they thought.

Instead, Jesus kneels and writes on the ground. What did he write in the dirt? Your guess is as good as mine. But when he stands, he gives the response no one was expecting: "Let him who is without sin among you be the first to throw a stone at her." He then kneels again and continues writing. What's interesting is how little attention he gives the Pharisees. He's not angry. He perfectly understands their point of view. What Jesus could see that the Pharisees couldn't was what was on this woman's heart. We'll never know why he chose to step in, or if she even repented, but God as man on earth saw enough potential in her to know she wasn't beyond repair. The law called for one thing, but God's grace called for another.

Word Study κυριεύω kyrieuō koo-ree-EW-oh G2961
to lord over to have dominion to be master of

From kyrios — Lord. "Sin will have no dominion over you" (6:14) uses this verb: sin will no longer lord over you. Paul is making a claim about ownership, not just behavior. Before Christ, sin was the ruling authority of a person's life. After baptism, a transfer of authority has occurred. The old lord no longer has jurisdiction. This is why Paul can tell the baptized to stop obeying sin — not because they're stronger, but because sin is no longer in charge.

Word Study βαπτίζω baptizō bap-TID-zoh G907
to immerse / to plunge under to submerge to bury in water

The word means to submerge — to plunge completely under. Paul's readers would have heard burial in this word. Baptism in Paul is not a ceremony marking a personal decision. It is participation in an actual death. You went under as one person. You came up as another. This is why Paul can speak in the past tense about dying to sin — because baptism literally already happened to you.

Word Study ἁμαρτία hamartia hah-mar-TEE-ah G266
sin (as a ruling power) missing the mark a governing force

Literally "missing the mark," but Paul doesn't use it that way in chapter 6. Here hamartia is a power — something that reigns, that you can be enslaved to, that pays wages. It's not a list of bad choices. It's a governing force with authority over a person's life. This matters because it explains why trying harder doesn't fix the problem. You don't outwork a power that owns you. You need to change masters.

Romans 6:3–4

"Do you not know that all of us who have been baptized into Christ Jesus were baptized into his death? We were buried therefore with him by baptism into death, in order that, just as Christ was raised from the dead by the glory of the Father, we too might walk in newness of life."

Church Father

"Baptism is burial — a burial not of a body into earth, but of sin into death. He did not merely say that we died, but that we were buried together; for burial is a more emphatic way of saying that death is certain and complete."

— St. John Chrysostom, Homilies on Romans, Homily XI (c. AD 391)

Commentary

The Catholic tradition insists on the objective reality of what happens at baptism. Baptism is not a symbol of a decision already made, or a public declaration of private faith. It is participation in Christ's death and resurrection — the old self genuinely put to death, the new person genuinely incorporated into the risen Christ. The sacrament does something, not just signifies something.

This is why Paul speaks in the past tense throughout chapter 6: "you were buried," "you were raised," "you died to sin." He is not describing aspirations. He is describing completed facts. The work of baptism is done. The ongoing Christian life is the process of believing and living out what is already objectively true.

CCC 1227–1228, 1265

Lectionary Connection

Romans 6:3–4, 8–11 is the second reading at the Easter Vigil every year — Year A, B, and C. It is read while the RCIA candidates are being baptized. Every Catholic who has ever attended an Easter Vigil has heard this passage proclaimed at the exact moment someone goes into the water. The Church places it there deliberately.

Movement Two
Romans 6:15–7:13

The Law Is Good. That's the Problem.

Sin seizes the commandment

"So the law is holy, and the commandment is holy and righteous and good." Romans 7:12

Rules are an interesting thing. Discipline to follow them is one of the greatest signs of a strong moral character. It shows that we trust that the rules are there for a reason and discipline shows that we have the willpower to say no when the path of least resistance would be to just say yes. We can all think of someone who has great discipline. For many of us it's a favorite athlete or maybe someone in the armed forces. People who wake up at the crack of dawn to go for a run in the dead of winter, not because it's what they want to do but because they know it will make them stronger. That discipline doesn't make them better than anyone else, at least not on its own, but small actions tend to add up to big results. Going for one run is great, but what makes the difference between a professional athlete and the average Joe is consistency.

Paul has already said that sin is dead and the law with it, but he follows up by saying that this does not mean the law was unholy. Instead, he calls us to go from being slaves to sin and instead become "slaves of God." That sounds pretty extreme at first blush but think about what he's saying with that phrase. A slave has little choice when it comes to the will of his master. If the master says go, they go. If the master says stay, they stay. The slave's desire doesn't matter and while we can all agree slavery is immoral when enslaved by man, being a slave to God is anything but.

Think for a moment about if you did everything God asked of you for a single day. If from the moment you opened your eyes to the moment you closed them at night, you did nothing but serve him. What would that look like? Now think about doing that day after day for the rest of your life. The weight of that responsibility seems impossible. What about work, friends, family, sports, leisure, entertainment? What would we be without them? That's where the problem with humans resides. We are not slaves to God. If we were, the world would be better for it, but that would take away the free will that God gave us to make a choice. The discipline to wake up day after day and do what God calls is not an easy task but it's what we're called to do.

Discipline is what Paul is calling for from us. The discipline to know that sin exists, to feel its temptation, and still say "no, that's not what God wants for me." Even the greatest athletes miss that morning run every now and then but for them that's an anomaly. It's okay to acknowledge that we're sinners but that doesn't mean it's okay to sin. What matters is that we dust ourselves off, repent, and aim to do better tomorrow. Christ's death gave us the Holy Spirit to walk with us and know right from wrong even when it's not explicitly written in a rulebook. The question is how often we listen and obey as slaves to God who wants nothing but goodness for us.

Word Study δοῦλος doulos DOO-los G1401
slave / bond-servant one owned by another one under absolute authority

Slavery is Paul's central metaphor across Romans 6:15–23. His readers lived in a slave economy — this word carried full weight. His point is that neutrality doesn't exist. Every person is oriented toward and serving something. The question is never whether you have a master — it's which master you have. The offer of the gospel isn't freedom from all obligation. It's a transfer from a master who destroys you to one who gives you life.

Word Study νόμος nomos NO-mos G3551
law (Torah) principle / rule governing standard

Paul is fighting on two fronts in chapter 7: against people who think the law saves, and against people who think the law is the villain. His position is neither. The law is good — it reveals God's will perfectly. It just cannot transform the will that receives it. Think of it this way: the law is a mirror, not a surgery. It shows you exactly what's wrong. It cannot fix it. That distinction is everything.

Word Study ἐπιθυμία epithymia ep-ee-thoo-MEE-ah G1939
coveting / craving disordered desire reaching for what is not yours

The tenth commandment — "you shall not covet" — is the hinge of Romans 7:7–11. Epithymia is the disordered desire that reaches for what belongs to someone else, or what God hasn't given. Paul says without the law naming it, he wouldn't have known it was a problem. The law turned a nameless itch into a diagnosed condition. Sin didn't cause the itch — but sin used the diagnosis to make the itch far worse.

Romans 7:10–11

"The very commandment that promised life proved to be death to me. For sin, seizing an opportunity through the commandment, deceived me and through it killed me."

From the Tradition

"The law is given not to justify but to show our need for justification. As a physician does not make a man sick by showing him his disease, so the law does not make men sinners by showing them their sins — but it does make sin manifest."

— Thomas Aquinas, Summa Theologiae I-II, Q. 98 (c. AD 1270)

Commentary

Paul's argument that we are freed from the law can sound — and has been read by some — as freeing Christians from moral obligation entirely. The Catholic tradition firmly rejects this. Freedom from the law as a mechanism of salvation is not the same as freedom from the law as a revelation of God's will.

The Old Law, including the Ten Commandments, is called in CCC 1963 a "preparation for the Gospel." It is not the law's fault that sin hijacked it. The moral precepts of the law — love of God, love of neighbor, the commandments — remain binding, not because following them saves us, but because they reveal the shape of a life lived in right relationship with God. Grace doesn't cancel the commandments. It makes keeping them possible for the first time.

CCC 1963–1964, 2068

Reflection — Movement Two

Paul calls us to become "slaves of God." If you tried that for one day — waking up and asking what God actually wanted from every decision — what would change first?

The law is good, but it can't do the thing you actually need. Where have you used discipline or rule-following to try to fix something that only grace can heal?

Romans 7:14–25

The Wretched Man

The cry before the answer

"Wretched man that I am! Who will deliver me from this body of death? Thanks be to God through Jesus Christ our Lord." Romans 7:24–25

Paul closes chapter 7 reflecting on sin the way many of us do: "For I do not do the good I want, but the evil I do not want is what I keep doing." We have all been in that rut before where we have to ask ourselves "why did I do that?" But what Paul is teaching us is that the very ability to ask "why do I do the things that hurt me?" is a blessing. This is the Holy Spirit working within us, telling us that sin goes against the goodness he wants for us. We rarely ever sin without knowing we're doing something wrong but that's the great thing about acknowledging it and reconciling with it. By looking back at your day and confessing what it is you did that you wish you hadn't, you also set yourself up to do better tomorrow. That doesn't excuse the sin, but it gives purpose to it.

Ultimately, the law is meant to be a mirror, not the end solution. By following the law and recognizing when we've strayed from the path, we can correct our course. The very fact that we feel shame is good. It means we know we're meant for more. But it's important not to let that shame consume you and give way to saying "well if I did it before, what's one more time?" That's why Paul calls us to serve the law of God with our innermost being. Not just to check the boxes of doing what the law says, but to lead us down a path toward goodness. Every step we take where we reject sin and accept God, we take one step closer to Him. Do that time and time again and the goodness it creates will become apparent and the sin won't look so tempting anymore.

The important thing to understand is that Christ did die for our sins. Following the law can't pay off our debts because that check was already written by Christ himself. But that doesn't mean we're free to do as we please. It is still the rejection of sin and the choice to instead say yes to God that opens us to receive more of what God continually offers, because it's what he wanted for us all along. It doesn't make us any better than the person in the pew next to us, but it makes us a better, more disciplined person that's stronger today than yesterday because we followed what Christ would want from us. The Holy Spirit will always speak within us to tell us right from wrong, but the discipline to acknowledge sin, weigh it, and say no to it is the training that will make us stronger defenders of faith and followers of Christ.

Word Study νοῦς nous NOOS G3563
mind / inner self the reasoning faculty the willing, knowing self

Paul says his nous — his inner man, his deepest reasoning and willing self — delights in God's law (7:22). This is the part of him that genuinely wants what is good. But it's at war with the flesh. Both sides are actually Paul. This is not possession by an outside force, and it's not dramatic spiritual warfare. It's the divided human will — knowing the good, wanting the good, and still failing to do it from the inside.

Word Study ταλαίπωρος talaiporos tah-LAY-poh-ros G5005
wretched / miserable exhausted and defeated worn down by endurance

Paul uses this word exactly once in all his letters. It is not the language of a bad day. It is the accumulated weight of losing the same battle over and over again — the sound of someone who has stopped performing and stopped pretending. The word originally described a person worn down by the burdens of hard labor. Paul uses it for the person who has been fighting the same internal war for years and finally runs out of ways to dress it up.

Word Study ῥύομαι rhyomai HROO-oh-my G4506
to rescue / to deliver to draw out of danger to snatch from harm

The verb behind "who will deliver me?" is a military rescue word — pulling someone out of active danger, not helping someone improve themselves. Paul is not asking for a better self-improvement strategy. He is asking to be dragged out of a war he is losing. The answer that follows in verse 25 is not a principle or a practice. It is a person. The rescue isn't self-accomplished. It comes from outside.

Romans 7:22–23

"For I delight in the law of God, in my inner being, but I see in my members another law waging war against the law of my mind and making me captive to the law of sin that dwells in my members."

Church Father

"I could not yet love you, because I was wavering between two wills — the old and the new — and they tore my soul apart. I understood from my own experience what I had read: 'The flesh lusts against the spirit, and the spirit against the flesh.'"

— St. Augustine of Hippo, Confessions, Book VIII (c. AD 397)

Commentary

The Church has a name for what Paul describes in Romans 7:14–24: concupiscence. It refers to the inclination toward sin that remains in a person even after baptism. Baptism removes the guilt of original sin. It does not remove the tendency. That tendency — the pull toward disordered desire, the preference for lesser goods over God — remains and has to be struggled with for a lifetime.

This is not a failure of baptism. It is the shape of the Christian life in the body before the resurrection. Paul is describing not someone without grace, but someone with grace who still experiences the pull. The Catholic tradition, following Augustine, holds that concupiscence itself is not sin — it becomes sin when we consent to it. The battle Paul describes is what the saints call the spiritual combat. It doesn't end until glory.

CCC 2515, 1264

Lectionary Connection

Romans 7:15–25 is the second reading on the 14th Sunday of Ordinary Time in Year A. It is one of the few lectionary appearances of the "wretched man" passage. Hearing it proclaimed at Mass — in the context of a community that knows exactly what Paul is talking about — changes the way the passage lands.

Reflection — Movement Three

"I do not understand my own actions." There's probably something in your life you keep doing that you hate doing, and you've tried to stop before. What does Paul's diagnosis here change about how you understand that struggle?

Paul ends chapter 7 with a cry before he gives the answer. He lets the problem land before he moves to the solution. Is there something you've been rushing past — pushing too quickly to the fix without sitting in the reality of the problem first?
