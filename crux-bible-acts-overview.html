The Story So Far

From Fear to Fearless Witness

What happened after the resurrection

Acts of the Apostles serves as the follow-up to Luke's Gospel about what Jesus did with what happened after. What happened that turned a group of terrified disciples who were so afraid of dying along with Jesus that they abandoned him as he hung on a cross, that turned them into a determined group of evangelists willing to die rather than dispute the resurrection.

The answer is, what happened was one of the most improbable (or miraculous) stories in human history. Within three centuries of the resurrection of Christ, a movement that had begun with twelve fishermen, tax collectors, zealots, and outcasts in a small Roman province eventually spread across the entire known world and transformed the Roman Empire itself into something new. Acts is the story of how that transformation started.

Acts doesn't start with a group of brave men ready to take on the world though. It begins with the disciples hiding in an upper room in Jerusalem. Their leader was just executed three weeks ago. With no money, power, political connections, and more importantly, no plan. They are waiting for something, simply because Jesus told them to wait.

The question Acts is answering is simple: if Jesus truly did rise from the dead, so what? How does that actually change how we should operate and worship from then on? The answer turns out to be.. everything, starting with the people who least expected it.

Now let's take a look at who actually wrote this book, who it was written to, when it was written, and why before diving into the book itself.

Author & Date

Luke, the Physician

Why we know the who and the when

Author: Luke, the Physician. Luke is not explicitly named as the author within the text of Acts but the Church has held from its earliest centuries that it was written as a follow-on by Luke to his Gospel. Luke was a physician and traveling companion of Paul (the same Paul who wrote most of the New Testament we read every week in church). He was also a Gentile which is important to note, since Acts is at its core, a story about the Gospel breaking out past Israel to the rest of the Gentile world, told by a Gentile.

Why are scholars so sure the author was Luke?

The "We" Passages. The strongest evidence for the author being Luke lies in grammatical tells from the text itself. Four times in Acts (16:10–17, 20:5–15, 21:1–18, 27:1–28:16), the narration shifts to saying "we" and every time it happens, it's during a part of a journey where Paul had been a traveling companion with Luke. Without getting too far into the weeds, scholars believe it is Luke writing this (not just another of Paul's companions) because the author of Acts switches back and forth from "they" (Paul and his companions) to "we" at the same points in Paul's journey where Luke and Paul met back up. Why it's Luke and not some other companion: the sharpest evidence is 20:4–5. Right before the "we" restarts, the text names a whole list of Paul's companions in the third person (Sopater, Aristarchus, Secundus, Gaius, Timothy, Tychicus, Trophimus) — "these went on and were waiting for us at Troas. But we sailed away from Philippi..." That line rules out every named companion as the narrator in one stroke. Whoever "we" is, it isn't any of the people just listed.

Commentary

  • 16:10–17 — Troas to Philippi, second missionary journey. The "we" starts the instant Paul has the vision of the Macedonian man ("Come over to Macedonia and help us," 16:9) — the very next verse says "we sought to go on into Macedonia." It drops back to "they" at 16:40, exactly when Paul and Silas leave Philippi — implying the author stayed behind there.
  • 20:5–15 — Philippi to Troas to Miletus, third missionary journey, the return leg. The "we" picks back up at the exact place it left off — Philippi — roughly 6–8 years later. Reads like the author never left, and rejoined Paul's group when Paul passed back through.
  • 21:1–18 — Miletus to Jerusalem via Tyre and Caesarea, ending with Paul's arrest.
  • 27:1–28:16 — the prisoner transport to Rome, including the shipwreck off Malta, ending with Paul under house arrest in Rome.

The timing lines up with Paul's own letters, too. Colossians 4:14 and Philemon 24 both name Luke as present during the exact Roman imprisonment where the last "we" passage ends — and 2 Timothy 4:11 ("Luke alone is with me") shows he stayed with Paul to the end.

Colossians 4:14

"Luke the beloved physician greets you."

The Audience. Both the Gospel of Luke and Acts were written to Theophilus (Luke 1:3, Acts 1:1). Luke calls him in his Gospel "most excellent," a title Acts itself later uses for Roman governors Felix & Festus (23:26, 24:3, 26:25). Most scholars read Theophilus as a real person, likely a Roman official or wealthy patron who funded the writing and its copying/circulation, which would have been significantly expensive at the time.

Paul's Writings. Colossians 4:14 ("Luke the beloved physician greets you") and Philemon 24 are both written during the exact Roman imprisonment where Acts' last "we" passage ends (28:16, house arrest in Rome). Then 2 Timothy 4:11 "Luke alone is with me" comes from Paul's final imprisonment, showing Luke stayed with him to the end. The overlap between Luke and Paul's writings is too significant to ignore.

When was Acts written? The honest answer is anywhere between 62 AD and 90 AD. The earlier end of the range being because Acts ends with Paul imprisoned before he was executed by the Romans in the mid-late 60s AD. There's also no mention of the fall of Jerusalem (70 AD). Both events seem like something that would have been worth mentioning, if they were written after the fact. Still, some of the Gospel of Luke reads as if using the Gospel of Mark as a source which, having been written ~70 AD, would mean Acts was written after and therefore, so would Acts. Either way, what's important to understand is that this book was written within the lifetime of a man who had lived alongside those who knew Jesus to people who could dispute his claims and still find living witnesses. This is not a book contrived centuries after the resurrection of Christ. It's a book that truly shows the events that unfolded directly after the crucifixion.

3
Why This Book Matters

Certainty, Not Legend

Why Luke wrote this down at all

Luke tells us directly in Luke 1:4 why this matters. Certainty. "so that you may know the certainty of the things you have been taught."

If you've ever read the book "Case for Christ" by Lee Strobel (or seen the movie) Luke may remind you a bit of Lee. He was a righteous man, sure, but he wasn't just setting out to retell a story that had already been told. He was actively searching for proof, funded by Theophilus to do so, to get to the root of the matter. Was Christ truly the son of God? Luke says yes and he says it with his chest.

What's also astounding about Acts is that the Romans found no fault in the teachings of the early Christians. Gallio (18:14-15) flat out tells the Jews who want to see Paul condemned that their teachings aren't political and have done nothing worth punishing. This matters because it means even the Romans could see that what we read in Acts wasn't just some political movement aimed at Christians gaining power for their own gain. It was a religious movement aimed only at spreading the good news that Christ is who he said he is and our eternity depends on him. Now, Paul was eventually executed by the Romans by Nero, but only as a scapegoat. Without giving an entire history lesson, he wasn't executed because of what he taught but rather because of the Great Fire of Rome that Romans blamed on the Christians although some believed Nero himself started the fire.

Commentary

Every time Paul is formally examined by a Roman authority in Acts — the Sanhedrin, then Felix, then Festus, then Herod Agrippa II — the charges against him collapse under actual legal review. Festus admits to Agrippa he has no real charge to send to Caesar (25:25–27). Paul is confident enough in this pattern to invoke his right as a Roman citizen and appeal directly to Caesar (25:11).

Paul's execution a few years later came from a different mechanism entirely. Tradition dates his death to c. AD 64–68 under Nero, tied to the aftermath of the Great Fire of Rome (AD 64). Tacitus — a Roman senator, no friend to Christians — records that Nero, facing rumors he'd started the fire himself, shifted blame onto Christians and had them executed brutally to redirect public anger, calling it scapegoating driven by "hatred of the human race" as pretext, not a legal verdict on Christian teaching (Annals 15.44). One detail underlines this: tradition holds Paul was beheaded rather than crucified, the more "dignified" execution reserved for Roman citizens — Rome honored his citizenship even in an extra-legal purge.

"Acts is a book not aimed at pushing a political agenda or making money for the early Church. It is a book that was written with one purpose, to prove to those who read it that they can be certain that the Gospels are true and Jesus Christ is our path to salvation."
